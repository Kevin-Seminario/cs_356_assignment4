# Agreement opportunity sweep after Round 2

This is a planning comparison, **not a live utility forecast**. I read the Round 2 switch logs, statements, and bilateral meters to identify selected routes and counterparties. I then made matched 10-second runs from the kit's full-ring practice world using the submitted switch for our AS and the starter for every other AS. Each world's model includes the direct relationships selected in our Round 2 log; one new agreement is added at a time. These models omit competitors' actual programs, hidden agreements, installed cross-ring hardware, and the next round's traffic/failures. World 2's modeled GPT 2 transit hits its $400 cap, whereas the actual Round 2 receipt was $89.28; the modeled absolute utilities should therefore **never** be read as predictions. The same-model differences are directional evidence only.

## Where routes were missing

- **World 1, our AS 23:** Ten local ASes were selected as direct peers. RedBull, the eleventh local neighbor, was selected as a provider. GPT 2 (AS 17) and GPT 1 (AS 25) already had free peer routes. There was no obvious unconnected local AI seat. Round 2 paid RedBull $75.584 for 94.48 Mb; the [matched RedBull comparison](redbull-peering-counterfactual.md) favored free peering by $36.44 without a crossing failure and $103.55 with one.
- **World 2, our AS 23:** Eight local direct peers plus GPT 2 (AS 25) as a paying transit customer. The selected-route log lacked AS 20 (Lossless Pilots in the earlier map) and AS 27 (Silent Falcons). GPT 2 paid us $89.28 in the live statement. Claude 2 (AS 17) already had a direct peer route.
- **World 3, our AS 15:** Eight local direct peers. The selected-route log lacked AS 9 (AI 2 in the earlier map), AS 10 (Anycast Badgers), and AS 14 (Net_doesnt_work). Claude 1 (AS 17) already had a peer route. World 3 had the highest actual Tier-1 charge, $1,547.64.
- **World 4, our AS 23:** Ten local direct peers plus far-side RedBull (AS 6). The selected-route log lacked local AS 27 (Silent Falcons). GPT 1 (AS 17) and Claude 1 (AS 25) already had peer routes. RedBull's free peer announced ASes 1 and 3 in addition to itself; changing it to paid transit would require evidence of genuinely additional useful routes.

An absent selected route is not proof that no offer or agreement exists now. Verify the current Agreements and Worlds pages by hand before sending a duplicate offer.

## Matched practice comparisons

- **World 3, add a free peer to AS 14:** +$578.33 utility versus the model baseline, comprising +$374.69 value and −$203.64 Tier-1 fees; on-time Calls rose from 22.24 to 29.52 Mb. With one of the two direct AS 14 IXP links failed from 5.4–7.2 seconds, the peer model still beat the no-peer model by +$515.52. This was the strongest single missing-neighbor case in the sweep, although the live amount is unknown.
- **World 3, add free peer to AI 2 / AS 9:** +$121.60. Offer **transit instead**, with AS 9 buying from us at $1/Mb and a $400 round cap, and the model showed +$476.52 to our utility, including $400 received. The modeled AI's own utility improved from −$120.24 with no direct agreement to $1,913.61 *after* the payment, because we offered it routes to our other peers. In a matched 5.4–7.2 second failure of their sole IXP link to us, our modeled transit gain remained +$472.09 versus no deal, and their utility remained above no-deal; the cap still bound. This is a plausible mutually beneficial offer, but we cannot observe its other live agreements or predict whether its AI policy accepts these terms. Do not submit free peer and paid transit simultaneously; compare one signed arrangement at a time.
- **World 3, add free peer to AS 10:** +$118.91. Adding all three missing World 3 peers together gave +$819.60, less than the sum of their separate gains. With AS 9 as a transit customer and ASes 10/14 as free peers, the model showed +$1,173.09 versus its baseline; this is a synthetic upper opportunity, not an expected live score.
- **World 2, add free peer to AS 20 or AS 27:** +$134.53 or +$133.04 separately. Both together gave +$262.30. The model's existing GPT 2 transit payment is capped at $400, unlike the actual $89.28 Round 2 receipt, so the live increments could be different.
- **World 4, add free peer to AS 27:** +$115.82 in a model that omits the installed cross-ring routes. This is a weaker estimate than the World 3 result because RedBull's live far-side route set was materially different from the model.

## AI and paid-agreement checks

The W1-like no-failure model also tested converting GPT 2 or GPT 1 from free peer to a $1/Mb transit customer. It raised our modeled utility by $183.19 or $309.93 respectively, but charged the buyer at the $400 cap and changed its routes. There is no evidence these already-peering AI teams would agree to pay; their live alternative is free. These are lower-priority negotiations than World 3's unconnected AI 2.

Adding a transit contract *on top of* an existing free peer with GPT 2 or GPT 1 left the W1-like routes and Tier-1 costs unchanged while collecting about $75 on the same traffic. That is a payment without demonstrated new service and can be flagged as a sham deal under R33. **Do not pursue the overlapping-contract tactic.** If proposing transit to a current peer, identify the additional destinations or service they actually gain, model both parties' utility, and avoid double-contract ambiguity.

Buying transit from an AI or cross-ring owner is justified only when it exports destinations not already reached through free peering and the incremental on-time value plus avoided Tier-1 fees exceed *all* bilateral transit payments. World 1 RedBull illustrates the danger: most billed bytes were for its own AS, not additional destinations. Paid peering is another option when traffic is asymmetric, but it mostly changes settlement, not reachability; it is lower priority than filling an absent route. Backup transit requires buyer failover **and** seller routing across its own network; the [backup test](backup-transit-test.md) showed it could hurt without seller cooperation.

## Practical order

1. Verify World 3 AS 14's current identity and whether an active route already exists; seek free peering there first. Its same-sector route affected on-time Calls, not merely Tier-1 expense.
2. Offer World 3 AI 2 / AS 9 legitimate transit at $1/Mb, $400 cap, one round. It can review offers after results and during its 8–9 a.m. Central daily action window (R2). If it declines, try free peering. The model suggests a gain for both sides, but acceptance and live traffic remain unknown.
3. Seek free peers with World 3 AS 10, World 2 ASes 20/27, and World 4 AS 27. Confirm current team names on the site; our naming evidence is older map screenshots, while the AS-number gaps come from Round 2 logs.
4. Revisit World 1 RedBull's $0.80/Mb transit: seek mutually signed free peering or a cheaper provider deal, and decline an unwanted renewal at the proper renewal cutoff. Keep the profitable World 2 GPT 2 transit unless new metering shows net harm.
5. Continue World 1 Jumbo Comets and any same-sector backup talks only after both buyer and seller can route the return path. Ask cross-ring owners for their actual offered destination set before paying for transit. R34 prohibits sharing switch programs with other teams; discuss desired forwarding behavior and test results instead.
